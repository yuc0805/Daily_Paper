# When Agents Slow Down: Understanding LLM Agents' Test-Time Strategies via Elo-per-token Analysis

**Arxiv:** 2609.15309 | **Date:** 2026-09-15 | **Area:** agent

## What it does
Proposes Elo-per-token analysis, a measurement protocol for open-ended tasks that produce continuous scores for intermediate submissions. It tracks the best solution found at each token budget and fits a Bradley-Terry model to aggregate within-task orderings into Elo ratings comparable across tasks with different score scales. Applied to four general-purpose agents on four open-ended benchmarks with sessions up to 100M tokens, and to three feedback-driven optimization harnesses under controlled single-task interventions. The central finding is that agents convert tokens into Elo faster than independent sampling early on, then fall below that reference.

## Delta from parents
Where LATS and DeepSeek-R1 each propose a way to spend test-time compute and report the score at the end of the run, this paper measures the derivative rather than the endpoint, because the endpoint hides where the spending stopped paying. The method needs a reference curve, and independent sampling supplies one that is theoretically characterized: Elo grows linearly with log compute. Against that reference the authors define the scaling inflection point, the per-session budget at which the agent's marginal Elo gain drops to the independent-sampling rate. Where Akyurek treats test-time adaptation as a single knob, this work makes budget allocation itself the decision variable and shows it is actionable: splitting 100M tokens across parallel sessions sized at the inflection point gained 264 Elo over one long session and 355 Elo over ten short ones on FrontierCS Polyomino Packing. The human comparison is the sharpest part — the strongest historical AtCoder Heuristic Contest contestants improve superlinearly over contest time on the same tasks, so the agent slowdown is a property of current agents, not of the tasks.

## Why Leo should care
The protocol is a measurement contribution, not an architecture, so it transfers to any long-horizon loop with a continuous intermediate score. That includes time-series and bio-sensing agent loops, where a forecasting or classification metric on a held-out split gives exactly the continuous intermediate signal the method requires, and where nobody has yet reported a scaling inflection point. It also supplies a defensible way to set compute budgets in a proposal: instead of asserting that more search helps, one measures where it stops helping and argues for parallel sessions at that width. The limitation to note is that the method needs continuously scorable intermediate submissions, which excludes most benchmark tasks with a single binary outcome, and the Elo aggregation inherits Bradley-Terry's assumption of transitive comparisons.
