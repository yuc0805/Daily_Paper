# SMELT: Scaling Laws for Compute-Matched MoE Looped Transformers

**Arxiv:** 2609.01343 | **Date:** 2026-09-02 | **Area:** moe

## What it does
Looped Transformers reuse a shared block of layers to increase effective depth without adding parameters, but the published comparisons hold model size fixed, so the extra forward passes bring extra FLOPs and the reported advantage mixes architecture with compute. This paper studies looping on sparse mixture-of-experts Transformers while matching three budgets at once: per-token FLOPs, total non-embedding parameters, and KV cache. The resulting recipe, SMELT, loops the middle half of the layers twice. Fitting a separate Chinchilla-style scaling law per architecture across four sizes up to 54B non-embedding parameters, SMELT saves 6.8 to 18.0 percent of training FLOPs on the compute-optimal frontier, and the downstream gain exceeds what the validation loss difference predicts.

## Delta from parents
Where the Transformer fixes depth by stacking distinct layers and the mixture-of-experts line buys capacity by adding experts that are not all activated, this paper asks what a second visit to the same layers is worth once nothing else is allowed to grow. The budget-matching is the contribution rather than the loop itself, because it converts a claim that was previously confounded into a measured scaling-law gap. Where DeepSeek-V3 and ST-MoE separate total parameters from activated parameters, SMELT adds a third separation, between parameter count and the number of times a parameter is used, and shows the two are not interchangeable at fixed FLOPs. The mechanistic part reports why: the second visit reduces the attention sink and moves attention mass onto content tokens, which is an inductive-bias claim rather than a capacity claim, and it predicts the observed pattern that the gain grows with sample length and with the number of in-context examples.

## Why Leo should care
The finding that the downstream gain exceeds what the loss gap predicts is the second instance in two weeks of loss and accuracy coming apart, after the Qwen3.8-Next report made the same point from the n-gram vocabulary side. Two independent architecture papers now say validation loss is the wrong selection criterion, which is worth carrying into any architecture sweep. The transfer to time-series is direct and unclaimed: physiological backbones are parameter-constrained because they run on device, so depth reuse is exactly the axis available when parameters are not, and the reported dependence on sequence length favours long recordings rather than short windows. HARMamba and Bi-Mamba+ in the library both add depth by stacking; neither tests reuse.
