# Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models II: One Training Example

**Arxiv:** 2609.04172 | **Date:** 2026-09-05 | **Area:** reasoning

## What it does
The paper examines the role of training data in on-policy distillation by training at the data-minimal limit, a single query. One-shot OPD keeps improving for hundreds of steps and recovers most of full-data OPD's gain across task domains and model families. The explanation is state coverage, the fraction of states full-data OPD visits that a query set's rollouts reach: one query already reaches 71.5 percent, most of it within the first 100 steps, and 16 semantically distinct queries reach 98.9 percent and match full-data training. Alignment slows at a similar rate whether OPD trains on one query or the whole dataset, so the method is data-overfed and algorithm-starved. Content-light templates and off-domain WildChat queries also approach the real-query baseline, separating task content from induced state coverage.

## Delta from parents
Where 2608.31046 removed the teacher and kept the data, this paper keeps the teacher and removes the data, and both recover most of the gain. Read together they say that the remaining contribution of on-policy distillation sits in the student's own rollout distribution and in the slow rate at which the student absorbs a fixed set of states, not in the teacher's per-token scores and not in dataset scale. Cliff sits on the other side of the same question: it accepts the teacher and improves the placement of the signal, so the three papers now bracket OPD from the data side, the supervision side and the credit-assignment side. The state-coverage measurement is the new instrument here, and it is what makes the one-query result an explanation rather than an anecdote.

## Why Leo should care
This is a well-instrumented ablation of a post-training method that is currently absorbing a large amount of compute, and it is the second paper in a week to find that a headline mechanism is not carrying the reported gain. The state-coverage metric is reusable: it is a general way to ask whether a training set is adding anything beyond the states the model would have visited anyway, which applies to any small-data domain, including health time series where labelled data is the binding constraint.
