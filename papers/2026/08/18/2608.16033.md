# R^3-Bench: LLMs Struggle with Resource-Rational Reasoning under Shared Budgets

**Arxiv:** 2608.16033 | **Date:** 2026-08-18 | **Area:** reasoning

## What it does
R^3-Bench evaluates whether a reasoning model can allocate one shared computation budget across six problems presented together, drawn from mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning, in both tool-free and agentic settings. The key control is an offline empirical oracle built from the same model's matched single-problem response curves, so the benchmark compares the model against its own demonstrated competence rather than against another model. Across 72 main-table cells covering six models, the oracle mean matches or exceeds the contest mean in every cell and is strictly higher in 71. Under moderate tool-free pressure, simply replaying the budget with equal allocation beats the model's own contest performance for four of six models, and trajectory diagnostics show little online strategy updating.

## Delta from parents
Where Chain-of-Thought and DeepSeek-R1 both assume that more reasoning tokens on a single problem yield more accuracy, this paper holds the total token budget fixed and asks how the model divides it, because in practice an agent faces a queue rather than one problem. Where SPRINT and LATS change the shape of the computation, parallel branches and tree search respectively, this benchmark changes the accounting, showing that models already possess the competence measured by the oracle and lose it at the allocation step. The cross-area bridge in Leo's library between DeepSeek-R1 and Chain-of-Thought, reasoning-via-reinforcement-learning against reasoning-via-prompting, is pushed further here in a direction neither covered: both branches optimise per-problem quality, and neither says anything about how to divide a budget across problems. The finding that a fixed equal-allocation scheduler beats the model's own choices in four of six cases is the sharpest version of that point.

## Why Leo should care
The framing is directly useful for any pipeline that runs one model over many inputs under a wall-clock or cost ceiling, which is the shape of most bio-sensing inference at scale. It also gives a clean methodological move worth copying: comparing a system against an oracle assembled from its own single-task response curves, rather than against a competitor. No time-series or bio-sensing analogue of this benchmark exists yet.
