# Chain-of-Experience for Continual LLM Improvement

**Arxiv:** 2608.18027 | **Date:** 2026-08-23 | **Area:** agent

## What it does
Chain-of-Experience treats test-time interaction as the unit of study: a model accumulates experiential traces across repeated attempts at a task, using either its own self-feedback or an environmental signal such as correctness or a public coding test pass rate, and the traces feed forward into the next attempt. The authors evaluate eight models including GPT-5, Gemini-2.5 Pro and Claude-4.5 Sonnet across math, coding and knowledge tasks. Iterating on experience beats the feedback-free baseline consistently, with a 5.6% overall gain and 19% lower API cost, and self-feedback alone already accounts for a large part of that. Combining feedback channels adds more, accuracy per token is higher than for existing test-time strategies, models stay robust when the feedback is weak or spurious, and most of the gain arrives in the early iterations.

## Delta from parents
Where chain-of-thought placed the reasoning trace inside one attempt and threw it away afterwards, Chain-of-Experience carries the trace across attempts and makes the accumulated record the input to the next one, because the interesting question is no longer whether a model can reason once but whether it improves when it is allowed to try again. Where LATS spent test-time compute on branching and backtracking within a single episode, this work spends it on sequential episodes and reports better accuracy per token than tree search, which is a direct cost claim rather than a capability claim. Where the memory survey catalogued mechanisms, this paper measures what a plain append-only experience log actually buys, and the 19% cost reduction alongside the 5.6% accuracy gain is the number that makes it worth reading.

## Why Leo should care
The relevant finding is not the headline gain but the shape of the curve: most improvement arrives early and the models tolerate weak or spurious feedback. Both properties matter for any setting where the feedback signal is noisy and expensive to obtain, which describes clinical and physiological labels well. This paper also sits next to the MemTrapBench result from yesterday's digest, which found that faithfully recorded and relevant memories can still degrade current-task performance; reading the two together is more informative than either alone, since they disagree about whether accumulated context helps.
