How coalition size shapes bill cosponsorship in the U.S. Congress

Bernardo Pereira, Giulia Preti, Andrea Santoro, Gianmarco De Francisci Morales et al. · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.27565

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

Legislation is built by coalitions, yet studied through pairwise ties. Using five decades of U.S. House and Senate cosponsorship (1973-2025), we represent each bill as a group. Prior ties to current members predict who joins better than ties to the sponsor, and group history (earlier co-appearance with pairs of those members) produces a larger likelihood gain than sponsor ties alone. Coalition formation therefore retains a social memory of earlier groups: cooperation is organized around relational histories embedded in the coalition already assembled, which sponsor-cosponsor ties alone cannot recover. Completed coalitions also recur and share subgroups more often than degree- and size-preserving nulls predict. Party sorting peaks in coalitions of a few dozen members; as Congress polarized, small coalitions retained their cross-party share while mid-size coalitions became more partisan. Women are over-represented chiefly in coalitions led by Democratic women, although same-gender sponsor matching is close to zero after adjustment.
