HEARTS: Benchmarking LLM Reasoning on Health Time Series

Yang AI Lab et al. · ICML 2026 · link · Appears in: Signal/Time Series

What

HEARTS is a benchmark for reasoning over health time series. It integrates 16 real-world datasets across 12 health domains and 20 signal modalities and defines 110 tasks grouped into perception, inference, generation, and deduction. Evaluating 16 state-of-the-art LLMs on more than 20K samples, it finds that LLMs underperform specialized models, lean on heuristics, and fail at multi-step temporal reasoning, with scores dropping as sequences lengthen and sampling frequency rises.

Why

Several recent systems claim that LLMs can reason over time series, but each reports wins on its own task set, so there was no common yardstick and no way to see where the ability actually breaks. Prior health-LLM work established that general models can read signals with few examples but did not measure how far that generalizes across domains, modalities, and sequence lengths.

How

The benchmark spans multiple input formats (text, image, raw file) for the same series, which lets it separate format effects from reasoning ability. It defines a taxonomy of four capabilities and grades models on more than 20K test samples, reporting a shared, model-agnostic difficulty ordering across domains and modalities. The finding is that format mainly shifts absolute performance while relative task difficulty stays fixed.

Solves vs predecessors

TS-Agent and ChatTS each reported wins on their own task sets, which made cross-method comparison impossible. HEARTS fixes this by giving one adversarial testbed across 12 domains and 20 modalities, so the failure modes (heuristic shortcuts, collapse on long high-frequency sequences) become measurable rather than anecdotal.

Method vs predecessors

Where the parent works are methods, HEARTS is an evaluation. It contributes a capability taxonomy and a multi-format protocol rather than a new model, and its main technical move is holding the underlying series fixed while varying input format, which isolates reasoning from presentation.
