Human mobility shapes how urban policy effects spread across cities

Bijin Joseph, Hamish Gibbs, Takahiro Yabe, Esteban Moro · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2610.02639

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

Urban policies are often evaluated assuming their effects decay with geographic distance. However, cities are systems of human movement, and policy effects propagate through individual mobility rather than spatial proximity alone. This study demonstrates that neglecting mobility can systematically distort estimates of how urban policies affect activity. Using large-scale mobility data, we estimate the causal effects of three transportation interventions in Boston and New York City using both conventional geographic and mobility-based definitions of exposure. Geographic designs detect no effects, or even negative ones. When exposure is defined through mobility networks, the same interventions reveal substantial behavioral change. Boston's free-fare program increases overall activity by approximately 16%, with more pronounced effects among lower-income communities. Open Streets programs significantly increase visits to nearby businesses, particularly food establishments. For congestion pricing, geographic approaches suggest a decline in activity in Manhattan, while a mobility-based perspective indicates no net reduction but a shift away from shopping and grocery destinations. Analysis across 138 metropolitan areas in eight countries further reveals that geographic and mobility-network distances diverge locally in every city, indicating that this discrepancy is a general characteristic of urban mobility. Collectively, these findings show that urban policies operate not only where they are implemented but where and how people move, so their impact must be measured through mobility rather than through space.
