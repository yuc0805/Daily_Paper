The directions of science: How we search the unknown

Nicola Cinardi · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.18349

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

Science is producing knowledge at unprecedented scale, while artificial intelligence promises to accelerate the generation of hypotheses, analyses, and scientific outputs further. Yet increasing the volume of research does not necessarily expand the range of directions science effectively explores. We argue that science can be understood as a collective search process in which finite resources are allocated across an evolving space of possible research trajectories. Careers, funding, organization, publication, attention, and technology act as interacting selection mechanisms that shape this allocation. This Perspective distinguishes the visible diversity of scientific activity from its effective diversity: apparently different researchers, institutions, or projects need not provide genuinely different routes of exploration or evaluation. Our principal contribution is to introduce cross-stage dependence, which occurs when selection processes operating at different stages of science are not fully independent in the grounds through which they favour scientific trajectories. We hypothesize that, under uncertainty, repeated filtering through overlapping criteria, information, prior selections, or technological infrastructure can reduce the capacity of science to maintain plausible alternatives and redirect effort when evidence changes their relative promise. Artificial intelligence makes this problem increasingly consequential because it can simultaneously expand scientific search capacity and participate across multiple stages of production and selection. The challenge is therefore not simply to produce more knowledge, but to preserve sufficiently distinct routes of exploration, verification and correction while retaining the ability to change direction.
