What Should an Experiment Measure? Combining a Short-Term Experiment with Confounded Observational Data to Identify Long-Term Treatment Effects

Kentaro Kawato · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.30599

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

Observational data often record long-term outcomes but do not identify long-term treatment effects when selection depends on unrecorded characteristics, while randomized experiments remove this selection but require years of follow-up. We ask what a short-term experiment should measure so that, combined with observational data, it identifies the average long-term treatment effect in the observational population. In our main setting, the observational data record a coarse post-treatment measurement of the unrecorded characteristics but not a detailed measurement that would remove confounding. In this setting, we derive three implications for what the experiment should measure. It should (1) collect the same coarse measurement, (2) measure the characteristics in enough detail that conditioning on this detailed measurement and recorded covariates removes confounding and leaves the coarse measurement uninformative about selection and the treatment-specific long-term outcome mean, and (3) distinguish no more levels of the characteristics than the coarse measurement can separate. We characterize the treatment effect through linear inverse problems based on exclusion restrictions and completeness, and thereby show that combining such an experiment with the observational data identifies the effect. We propose an estimator that is based on the efficient influence function and removes the first-order effect of nuisance estimation errors. We give conditions under which this estimator is asymptotically normal and attains the semiparametric efficiency bound. Cross-fitting relaxes the conditions on nuisance estimation. In simulations and a Job Corps illustration, the proposed Tikhonov-penalized series estimator has smaller absolute bias and coverage closer to the nominal level than competing estimators.
