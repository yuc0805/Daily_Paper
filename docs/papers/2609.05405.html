WearableQA: A Benchmark for Health Reasoning over Real-World Wearable Data

Lee, Chen, Chuang, Shenoy, Wei, Ko, Kim, Corda (Meta) · arXiv preprint 2026 · arXiv:2609.05405 · Appears in: LLM-Health

What

A question-answering benchmark built on the actual longitudinal records of 200 real people, each carrying up to 500 days of daily wearable measurements alongside blood biomarkers and demographics. It contains 4,084 ten-option multiple-choice questions sorted into 16 types along two axes: data reasoning versus health reasoning, which separates computation over the series from physiological interpretation, and single-signal versus cross-signal reasoning, which separates reading one stream from combining several. Fourteen proprietary and open-source models score between 19.6 and 72.9 percent against a 10 percent chance floor, and most land below 60 percent.

Why

The claim that language models can reason over wearable data has been made repeatedly, but the evidence has come from evaluations that either use engineered features and short windows or use clean synthetic prompts. Neither setting contains what a deployed device actually produces: sensor noise, missing stretches, and between-person variability large enough that a population-level rule fails on an individual. Existing benchmarks therefore cannot distinguish a model that reasons over a person's record from one that recites a population prior, and they cannot say whether a failure came from arithmetic or from physiology.

How

Questions are generated by a dual-grounding procedure. Each candidate item must be supported both by a published physiological finding and by a statistically validated pattern present in the population data, so the answer key does not rest on an annotator model's judgment. The 16-type schema is the diagnostic instrument: because data reasoning and health reasoning are separated, and single-signal is separated from cross-signal, a per-type score says where a system fails rather than only how often. Authentic wearable distributions are preserved rather than cleaned, so device noise and inter-individual variability remain in the inputs.

  • Health-LLM (Kim 2024)The first systematic evaluation of language models on wearable-derived health prediction, built from curated features rather than raw longitudinal records.

Solves vs predecessors

Liu 2023 and Kim 2024 both measured whether the capability exists under favorable conditions. The failure mode they cannot surface is the one that matters in deployment: a model that answers correctly by applying a population average, and would answer identically for a different person with the same demographics. WearableQA fixes this by anchoring every question to one individual's own 500-day record and by keeping the noise, so a population prior is no longer sufficient to score well. The second failure mode it fixes is diagnostic blindness. A single accuracy number over a mixed question set cannot say whether a model failed to compute a rolling statistic or failed to know what that statistic means physiologically; the two-axis schema separates them.

Method vs predecessors

Health-LLM evaluated on engineered features over short windows, which turns the task into feature-vector-to-label mapping. WearableQA gives the model the raw longitudinal record and requires it to locate the relevant stretch before computing over it, which is a different and harder operation. GLOBEM contributed data and a prediction task; this paper contributes a question schema on top of comparable data, and the schema is the substantive addition. The dual-grounding construction also differs from the usual model-generated-item pipeline: requiring both literature support and population-statistical support means an item survives only if the relationship it tests is real in the world and present in this cohort, which removes a large class of unanswerable or ambiguous questions before evaluation.
