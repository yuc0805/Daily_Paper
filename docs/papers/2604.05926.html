FEEL: Quantifying Heterogeneity in Physiological Signals for Generalizable Emotion Recognition

FEEL authors · arXiv 2026 · arXiv:2604.05926 · Appears in: Signal/Time Series

What

FEEL is a large benchmarking study of emotion recognition from electrodermal activity (EDA) and photoplethysmography (PPG) across 19 public datasets. It evaluates 16 models spanning classical machine learning, deep learning, and self-supervised pretraining, grouped into four modeling families, with both within-dataset and cross-dataset tests that measure how well models hold up across experiment settings, devices, and labeling schemes.

Why

Emotion recognition from physiological signals is promising for mental health and emotion-aware systems, but there has been no standardized, large-scale evaluation across heterogeneous datasets. Without it, reported gains may reflect a single dataset rather than transferable skill. FEEL exists to measure generalization directly and to see which model family holds up when the device, setting, or labels change.

How

FEEL fixes a common pipeline and runs all four model families on the same 19 datasets, reporting arousal and valence classification under within-dataset and cross-dataset splits. Fine-tuned contrastive signal-language models score highest, but classical models such as Random Forests, LDA, and MLP stay competitive, and handcrafted-feature models beat raw-signal models in noisy, low-resource settings. Models trained on real-life data transfer well to lab settings (F1 = 0.79) and to constraint-based settings (F1 = 0.78).

Solves vs predecessors

The Gu 2025 survey describes biosignal foundation models in the abstract, and HeAR and SSL for HAR each report on their own data, so none answers whether one family transfers across datasets. FEEL fixes that by measuring all families on 19 datasets with cross-dataset splits. Its concrete result — that handcrafted features beat raw-signal models under noise and that classical models stay competitive — corrects the assumption that larger self-supervised pretraining always wins for physiological signals.

Method vs predecessors

FEEL is a benchmarking study, not a new model, so its contribution is the cross-dataset evaluation design. Compared to HeAR and SSL for HAR, which propose representations, FEEL compares those representations against classical and feature-based baselines on shared data. Compared to the Gu 2025 survey, it replaces description with measured transfer numbers.
