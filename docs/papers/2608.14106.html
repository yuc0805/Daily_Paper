Forecast Collapse in Time-Series Foundation Models

Wan, Ma, Zhu, Liu, Wang, Wen, Liu · arXiv 2026-08-14 · arXiv:2608.14106 · Appears in: Signal/Time Series

What

The paper documents a failure mode it names forecast collapse. When time-series foundation models forecast hourly returns for 1,000 US equities, their predictions become nearly flat and rank the series poorly, as measured by cross-sectional correlation. The same models on the same panel do not collapse when the target is trading volume, which isolates target predictability rather than model family as the cause. The study spans time-series foundation models, twelve deep-learning forecasters and 97 public benchmark configurations, and introduces CalibRank, an objective that balances calibration against ranking. On the released Finance1K panel, CalibRank nearly triples cross-sectional correlation while keeping forecast amplitude close to the target, and improves correlation on every model tested.

Why

Time-series foundation models are trained and evaluated one series at a time. That convention is inherited from single-series forecasting and carries an assumption that does not hold for panels: that a model calibrated on each series individually is useful for decisions that compare series. Most downstream decisions on a panel are comparative rather than absolute, so the quantity that matters is the ordering across entities, and per-series metrics cannot measure it. Prior work has scaled pretraining corpora and architectures without touching this, so the failure has stayed invisible.

How

Two mechanisms are separated. First, low target predictability caps the amplitude of any calibrated point forecast, because the conditional mean of a barely predictable target sits close to a constant, so squared-error training drives the forecast flat by construction rather than by underfitting. Second, per-series objectives leave cross-series structure unidentified, so nothing in training pressures correct ordering. Optimising cross-sectional correlation on its own fixes ordering but inflates forecast amplitude by more than an order of magnitude, which is the calibration-ranking tradeoff. CalibRank combines the two terms in a single objective and is applied on top of existing models rather than requiring a new architecture.

Solves vs predecessors

Chronos and Moirai both demonstrate that a single pretrained model can forecast unseen series without fitting them, and both are scored with per-series error. The failure mode this paper identifies is invisible under that scoring: a model can be well calibrated on every series and still produce an ordering across series that is close to useless. Because the collapse is tied to target predictability rather than to architecture, enlarging the pretraining corpus in the Chronos or Moirai direction does not remove it. The paper therefore fixes something the predecessors could not detect, and does so by changing what the model is asked to optimise rather than what it is made of.

Method vs predecessors

Chronos discretises a series into tokens and trains a language-model-style next-token objective per series; Moirai keeps a probabilistic per-series likelihood over multivariate inputs. Both objectives factorise across series, which is exactly the property that leaves cross-series structure unidentified. CalibRank does not change the backbone or the tokenisation. It adds a cross-sectional ranking term to the per-series calibration term, so the loss no longer factorises across entities. That makes it applicable to any of the models in this lineage through fine-tuning, at the cost of requiring panel-structured training batches rather than independently sampled series.
