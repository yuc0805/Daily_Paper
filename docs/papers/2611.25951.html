Measuring climate credibility? Talk, emissions, and corporate debt composition

Georgios Chortareas, Fangyuan Kou, Alexia Ventouri · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.25951

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

We examine whether a directional measure that compares emissions growth with earlier climate discussion is associated with corporate debt composition. The analysis links earnings-call climate language, LSEG/Refinitiv emissions, Capital IQ debt shares, RepRisk incidents, and accounting information for public firms over 2010--2021. The Emissions-to-Talk Index (ETI) is the central empirical measure, but we describe it literally rather than treating it as a general indicator of disclosure quality. In the baseline industry-and-year fixed-effect specification, a higher ETI is associated with a lower bank-debt share. The estimate falls by about four-fifths and is statistically insignificant when firm and year fixed effects are used on the same observations. It is also insignificant when the completed index is used to predict debt composition one calendar year later. Results based on the separate talk and emissions components, a ratio-free interaction, and complete talk--emissions categories lead to the same broad conclusion. Oster and Frank sensitivity calculations, alternative covariance estimates, exact year-sign bootstrap calculations, and leave-one-year-out estimates show that the contemporaneous result is sensitive to specification and inference. We also report exploratory comparisons by competition, financial health, opacity, and period; they do not show a consistent within-firm pattern. DealScan tests using unique facilities likewise show no common relation across facility incidence, spreads, maturity, security, and covenants. The evidence supports an association in the baseline industry-and-year specification, but not a causal claim that firms choose less bank debt to avoid monitoring or that lenders restrict credit because of the index.
