When Do Foundation Models Pay Off? A Break-Even Analysis of Pretrained Time Series Forecasters

Tan Jerome, Simon · arXiv 2026 · arXiv:2607.04919 · Appears in: Signal (Time Series)

What

The paper measures when a pretrained time-series foundation model beats a classical forecaster as a function of task-data size. Across 30 benchmark datasets it compares zero-shot and LoRA fine-tuned foundation models (Chronos, Moirai, Lag-Llama) against Naive, ETS, ARIMA, and XGBoost at six training fractions from 2 to 100 percent, and reports the break-even sample count for each dataset.

Why

Practitioners face a binary choice with asymmetric cost: deploy a foundation model, which needs GPU infrastructure and has opaque failure modes, or keep XGBoost, which runs on CPU and is interpretable. Prior foundation-model papers reported aggregate zero-shot wins but did not tell a practitioner at what data volume the choice flips, so the decision was made on intuition rather than a measured threshold.

How

For each dataset the authors sweep training-set size and fit scaling curves for foundation models and classical baselines, then locate the break-even sample count where the ranking changes. They correlate break-even with dataset features (length, seasonality strength) and extract a training-free rule: series with fewer than 700 points and non-negligible seasonality should use the foundation model zero-shot without fine-tuning. They report that LoRA fine-tuning degrades accuracy on short series.

Builds on

  • ChronosOne of the three foundation models evaluated; tokenizes a series and forecasts with a language-model backbone.
  • MoiraiOne of the three evaluated; a masked-encoder universal forecaster trained across domains.
  • PatchTSTPatch-transformer forecaster representing the supervised neural baseline the foundation models are measured against.

Solves vs predecessors

Chronos and Moirai each argued their own case with aggregate zero-shot scores, which hide the data volume at which a classical model catches up. This paper fixes that failure mode by reporting per-dataset break-even points rather than a pooled win rate, so the output is an actionable threshold instead of an average.

Method vs predecessors

Instead of proposing a new architecture, the method fixes the decision procedure: measure scaling curves, locate the crossing point, and reduce it to a two-feature rule (length and seasonality) plus an optional small pilot. Where the parents contribute forecasting models, this work contributes the protocol for choosing between them.
