The Phases of Hierarchy

Gerard McCaul · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.33566

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

We develop a dynamical phase theory of hierarchy, in which depth decides whether a population of size $N$ that revises its own environment can correct it without overcorrecting. The statistics that let a large majority detect a small bias - the basis of the Condorcet jury theorem - also make its response grow as $\sqrt{N}$. Fed back into the environment it is judging, this sharpening destabilises the matched state through a flip bifurcation at a critical loop gain, which we obtain analytically for a class of models we term adaptive normal forms. We show that this instability can be attenuated by hierarchical layering, requiring a depth scaling logarithmically with $N$. This recovers known logarithmic bounds in organisational economics directly from dynamic stability conditions. Hierarchical depth carries its own costs however, and by analysing the signal chain transmission up the hierarchy we show that beyond a finite depth no correction reaches the top before its deadline, and that below it the cost of arrival grows exponentially with depth. A hierarchy that is too flat therefore oscillates and one that is too deep cannot be steered in time, leaving a bounded window of depth between an unstable flat phase and a frozen inertial phase. We further show that these results can be generalised by treating each layer as a real-space renormalisation step, classifying aggregation rules by how they transmit bias. This establishes a connection between these dynamic results to attention in Transformers, where a residual update is diffusion on the token graph and depth plays the role of scale. Finally we show these phase boundaries are observable in finite sampling, and propose measurements which may characterise the proposed phases in real adaptive systems.
