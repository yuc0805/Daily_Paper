How Fast Stored Household Assignments Go Stale: Evidence from North Carolina Voter Rolls

Prashant Chaudhary · 2026 · Explore · arXiv:2611.32091

Why recommended

Picked for exploration — from another field, outside your usual research areas.

Abstract

Household files and identity graphs are judged by whether they still find the person. That test misses what they store. A household assignment can go stale while every person-level link in it stays right, because the people around the person change. We measure how fast, for households derived from a voter register, by following North Carolina registrants on a persistent identifier across 40 distinct snapshots from November 2008 to November 2026 (3.6 million clients at the 2016 build in 60 counties). Eight years after the 2016 build, the graph finds the address of 0.831 of registrants still listed, yet its household is exactly right for only 0.322 of those found (0.309 reading stale listings as departures). A mover's stored household is right about one time in five at every build from two to eight years, so the loss runs through who stays and how households that stay change. Stricter thresholds of a probabilistic name-and-address matcher raise accuracy only to about the stayers' currency, mainly by selecting stayers. Lookup tables of currency by build-time features cut Brier score by 16% to 19% against evidence age alone and 6% to 10% against evidence age and household size, on held-out counties and a later file. A multistate model of household change does not beat a table with the same inputs, and the tables choose which quarter of a file to refresh no better than evidence age and household size do. In Ohio and Washington at eight to nine years the tables keep their ranking after an in-sample intercept shift; the accounting's level holds in Washington and not in Ohio. Simulated build error of 5% to 21% costs 2% to 10% of eight-year currency.
