# Reading Discipline — Daily AI Paper Routine

This is the routine you said you would follow. Pin this. Refer to it when the digest lands at 5 AM.

## The volume target

One deep read, three TLDRs, eight headline scans. Total time budget under one hour per day. This is not an arbitrary number. Real-world surveys of PhD reading habits show that productive researchers read around seventeen items per month on average, which is well below one paper per day. Cognitive science work on focused attention puts the human ceiling for deep, focused work at about four hours per day. You should not spend that entire budget on paper reading, because deep work is also where your own research happens.

A budget of one hour per day for reading leaves you three hours of deep work for your own research, plus the rest of the day for meetings, writing, teaching, code, and life. If you exceed the one-hour budget for reading, you are stealing from your own research time. That is the wrong trade.

## The three rules that prevent overwhelm

**The reading budget rule.** One deep paper (about twenty minutes), three TLDRs (about ten minutes), eight scan headlines (about five minutes). If you have not finished Tier A by six in the morning, stop and move on. The world will not end. The paper will still be there tomorrow, and if it really matters, you will see it again in next week's digest or hear about it from a colleague.

**The open-tab rule.** Maximum three papers open at any time. When you want to open a fourth, you must first close one. This forces you to decide whether the paper you are about to open is more important than something already open. Tab hoarding is the single most common failure mode for researchers trying to keep up.

**The two-page test.** If the first two pages of a Tier A paper do not justify the read (clear problem statement, novel contribution, credible setup), drop it. Promote a Tier B paper into the deep-read slot for that day. Sunk-cost reading is the second most common failure mode.

## What to read deeply versus what to skim

Read deeply only the Tier A paper. By design there is one. You should read the abstract, introduction, method, results, and limitations carefully. Take notes. If the method touches your work, sketch the idea on paper. If you find yourself reading the related work section in depth, you are off-budget and should stop.

For Tier B, the TLDR is the entire reading. Do not open the PDF unless the TLDR genuinely surprises you or directly threatens your current project. If you do open it, time-box to ten minutes and only read the introduction and the results figure.

For Tier C, the title and one-line hook are the entire reading. Open only if the hook hits something specific you are working on right now. If you find yourself opening more than one Tier C paper per day on average, your hooks are too long and you should ask for shorter ones.

## The time-series and bio-sensing rule

You said you do not want method-level detail for time-series and bio-sensing papers. The rule is: for every TS or bio-sensing paper, ignore the method itself, and only ask one question — what did they take from CV or NLP, and is that low-hanging fruit now gone? The digest will track this for you in a dedicated section called "Gap Watch."

The Gap Watch section will also flag prominent CV and NLP methods from the day that have not yet been ported to time-series or bio-sensing. These are your research opportunities. Treat them as a watchlist, not a to-do list. When you see a method appear in the "Unported opportunity" column three or four times in a month, that is a signal it is ripe for porting and you should consider it for your own work.

## Handling backlog and bad days

If you miss a day, do not try to catch up. Read tomorrow's digest tomorrow. The whole point of the budget is that one missed day is recoverable; a week of doubling up is not.

On quiet research days the digest will explicitly say "Quiet day" at the top and shrink the tiers. On those days, use the freed time to read one paper from the previous week's backlog rather than reading more of today's papers. Spacing your reading across days improves retention more than reading more papers per day.

## What to do with the digest after reading

Spend exactly five minutes at the end of the hour deciding: does anything you read today change a decision in your current project? If yes, write one sentence in a "Decisions changed by reading" log. If no, that is also a valid answer, and writing "no change" reinforces the habit of reading critically rather than reading for its own sake.

If after a month you find that almost every entry in the log is "no change," your reading list is probably too far from your work and you should narrow the topic filter on the digest.

## When to break the rules

Three legitimate reasons to exceed the one-hour budget. First, a Tier A paper that is directly on your project and clearly novel — you may spend up to two hours on it that day, but you must then skip the next day's deep read. Second, a method you want to port to time-series or bio-sensing — you may spend additional time reading the original CV or NLP paper, capped at one extra hour, and only twice per month. Third, a paper from a collaborator or a paper you are formally reviewing — separate budget, not counted here.

For everything else, the budget holds. Discipline is the point.
